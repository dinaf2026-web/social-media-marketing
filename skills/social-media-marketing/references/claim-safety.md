# Claim Safety Gate

Apply this gate before approving external-facing marketing copy.

## Non-negotiable rule

Never invent, strengthen, or imply factual support that is not present in user-provided material, connected sources, or verified research.

This includes:

- product capabilities or specifications;
- medical, health, wellness, diagnostic, treatment, safety, or efficacy claims;
- legal or compliance claims;
- financial, investment, earnings, savings, or ROI claims;
- scientific findings;
- environmental or sustainability claims;
- rankings, awards, certifications, approvals, or endorsements;
- prices, discounts, inventory, deadlines, scarcity, or availability;
- customer counts, download counts, followers, reach, conversion rates, retention, or other metrics;
- testimonials, reviews, customer stories, before/after results, or quotes;
- comparisons such as “better,” “safer,” “faster,” “#1,” “leading,” “most,” or “only.”

When a required check could not be run, name the check and the claim it leaves unsupported. Do not reason around the gap. An absent check is a statement about what was examined, never evidence that the claim is safe.

## Source status and claim status

Track two separate classifications for every material factual claim.

### Source status

1. **User-provided:** Stated by the user but not independently verified. Attribute it when relevant.
2. **Retrieved source:** Found in an uploaded or connected file. Record the file and the exact passage, table, or field relied on.
3. **Externally verified:** Checked during this task against a named current source. Record the source and access date.
4. **Assumption:** A working premise needed to proceed. Label it when it could affect the result.
5. **Creative proposal:** A marketing idea, positioning direction, hook, or concept. Not a factual source.

### Claim status

1. **Supported:** The exact claim is supported by an appropriate source, and the wording is no broader than the evidence.
2. **Needs qualification:** Some support exists, but the wording, scope, certainty, population, comparison, or time period is too broad.
3. **Unsupported:** Adequate support is missing. Remove it, replace it with non-factual positioning, or use a visible verification placeholder.
4. **Subjective creative statement:** Brand language that a reasonable viewer would not understand as an objective claim.

User-provided and connected material may establish what the brand says or has approved. They do not, by themselves, independently verify an objective claim.

For each supported material claim, keep an internal record of the source, exact supporting passage or data field, access date, and any limit on scope or freshness.

## Evidence-fit rules

Support must match the claim being made.

For health, science, safety, efficacy, and product-performance claims, check whether the evidence matches the exact product or formulation, ingredient amount, route or method of use, population, comparison, outcome, and time period.

Do not use:

- ingredient evidence as finished-product evidence;
- laboratory or animal research as proof of a human outcome;
- preliminary or observational research as proof of causation;
- research on one dose, formulation, or population as proof for another;
- a general disclaimer to preserve a headline that overstates the evidence.

Use current official sources for regulatory status, approval, certification, and program eligibility.

Check prices, discounts, inventory, deadlines, scarcity, and availability at the relevant source during the task. Record the date and time. If freshness cannot be checked, omit the claim or use a visible placeholder.

Use testimonials, reviews, quotations, and customer stories only when the exact source is available. Preserve the meaning. Confirm attribution, permission, and any incentive or material connection. Do not imply that one result is typical unless adequate support exists.

## Sensitive categories

For health/wellness content:

- do not diagnose;
- do not prescribe individualized treatment;
- do not promise outcomes;
- do not convert preliminary research into established clinical fact;
- distinguish research-use products from approved medical products when relevant;
- use “ask your doctor/qualified healthcare professional” language when the content could otherwise be read as individualized medical guidance.

For legal or financial content, do not market uncertain general information as personalized professional advice or guaranteed outcomes.

## Testimonials and social proof

Never fabricate a customer, quote, review, case study, UGC post, star rating, influencer endorsement, or before/after result. If a concept would benefit from social proof but none is supplied, write a placeholder such as `[insert verified customer quote]` or recommend collecting proof. Never turn a composite, paraphrase, or hypothetical person into an apparent real customer.

## Competitive claims

Require support for objective superiority. When support is missing, change:

- “the fastest” → “built for a faster workflow” only if the product design supports that framing;
- “better than X” → identify a specific design difference without claiming superiority;
- “guaranteed results” → describe intended use or user-controlled process.

## Numbers

Never invent numbers for rhetorical impact. Do not create fake percentages, survey results, “X times faster” claims, audience sizes, savings, or timelines.

## Commercial relationships and rights

Identify sponsorships, affiliate relationships, free products, paid endorsements, or other material connections. Verify current disclosure requirements when they affect the content. Do not hide a required disclosure in hashtags or vague language.

Do not assume the brand has permission to use customer content, music, footage, likenesses, trademarks, or third-party creative. Flag the required permission or license when it is not established by the supplied material.

## Final safety pass

Before final output, ask internally:

- What statements would a reasonable viewer interpret as factual?
- What evidence supports each material factual claim?
- Is the source user-provided, retrieved, externally verified, assumed, or creative?
- Did the copy become stronger than the source?
- Does a visual concept imply a result that the words carefully avoid claiming?
- Is a disclaimer being used to excuse a misleading headline? If yes, fix the headline.

When material support is missing, prefer truthful persuasion over hype.
